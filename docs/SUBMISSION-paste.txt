=== Project name ===
Trade Space: guess like an engineer, fly what NASA flew, design what NASA hasn’t

=== High-level summary ===
Trade Space is a browser game where you design a complete space mission and every number is real physics. Before the game reveals a number, you estimate it (orbit period, battery size, signal delay, data rate) and get a medal for how close your engineer’s gut was, then a "Why?" card shows the formula with your own numbers plugged in. You can replay history (SMAP 2015, Juno 2011) against NASA’s real design, real solar storms from NASA DONKI and the real events of each mission, or design the future: NASA’s top-priority Uranus Orbiter & Probe, with launch windows computed from real JPL Horizons planet positions. After each flight, the game searches every possible design (up to 27,000) and shows the Pareto frontier, proving there is rarely a single right answer.

=== Project details ===
**What it does.** Players pick a mission, hire a four-person crew whose members each bring one edge, make engineering decisions (orbit, instruments, power, communications, trajectory, rocket), watch a live budget of mass, cost, power and data, and fly the mission through a timeline. Three missions:
- **SMAP (2015), tutorial:** low Earth orbit, eclipse and battery sizing, radiometer resolution vs swath, downlink. The 26 geomagnetic storms of 2015 come from NASA DONKI, including the St. Patrick’s Day storm six weeks after launch, plus SMAP’s real radar failure on 7 July 2015.
- **Juno (2011):** solar power at 1/27 sunlight, Earth-flyby trajectory vs direct launch, polar orbit vs radiation belts, a real X-band link budget. Juno’s real history plays out: Earth flyby, Jupiter arrival, the 2016 valve problem, the 2019 eclipse-dodging burn.
- **Uranus Orbiter & Probe (future):** pick the launch year from a window chart built from real Jupiter and Uranus positions (JPL Horizons). RTG decay, a 19 AU link budget, the probe, aerocapture risk, and arriving before Uranus’s ~2049 equinox to see moon hemispheres Voyager 2 never saw.

**How it teaches.**
1. *Engineer’s Gut*: numbers stay locked as "??" until you estimate them on a log slider (orders of magnitude are what matter). Three levels play the same missions with the same physics and differ in the help on each estimate: *Explorer* gets a 3-step clue ladder, the formula and a calculator that fills in the design’s numbers; *Student* gets the formula and does the working out; *Engineer* gets no help, types a number and is graded strictly. Stars are kept per level.
2. You size the solar array and battery yourself, and the real requirement is revealed only in flight, so a low guess drains the battery on the first night.
3. Failures are paired with real history (Opportunity’s dust storm, Galileo’s stuck antenna, the Starlink storm losses).
4. *Ghost comparison*: your design next to NASA’s, with "what NASA chose and why".
5. The *Pareto frontier*: an exhaustive search of every design (16k for SMAP, 3k for Juno, 27k for Uranus) plots cost vs science and tells you if a cheaper design beats yours.

**Validity.** The physics is standard and unit-tested: Kepler’s laws, the cylindrical-shadow eclipse model, the diffraction limit, the rocket equation, the inverse-square law, a free-space link budget and Hohmann transfers. Calibrated checks: NASA’s real SMAP choices give 867 kg (real 944 kg); Juno gives 582 W at Jupiter (real ~500 W) and 3,432 kg (real 3,625 kg). For Uranus, the search alone, with no hints, converges on NASA’s concept study: 2031 launch, Jupiter assist, 3 RTGs, probe, Falcon Heavy. Part masses and prices are simplified and labelled as such in the game.

**Extras.** A live Voyager 1 strip interpolates JPL Horizons data to show its one-way light time crossing one full light-day this week.

**Tools.** TypeScript, React 19, Vite, Tailwind CSS, Vitest. Runs entirely in the browser, with no account and no download.

**Impact.** Students rarely get to experience a whole mission from concept to operations. Trade Space makes the trade-offs tangible in about 10 minutes per mission, works on any laptop or phone, and every number can be traced to its source.
